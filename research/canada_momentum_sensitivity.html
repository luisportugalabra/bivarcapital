Canada MomentumParameter sensitivity analysis

We analyzed the Canada TSX momentum market: one-at-a-time sensitivity sweeps across lookback window, market cap filter, portfolio size, regime window, volatility filter and weighting scheme, behind the Canada TSX momentum strategy. TSX (.TO), EODHD daily data, 2000–2026.

The engine is audited for lookahead bias: every signal uses only data available as of its decision date, and each month's return is generated by the position decided using the prior month's information, never its own.

Baseline for every sweep below (Sections II–VII)

Read this before the tables below. Every table in Sections II–VII shares one baseline: N=15, 12-month momentum (no skip), mcap ≥ 30th percentile, excludes top 25% most volatile, MA75 regime, equal weighted, CDRs and preferred shares excluded, since 2000 (319 months) — that baseline row reads CAGR +33.3%, Sharpe 1.312, MaxDD −26.1% in each table below, and matches Section X, Final Configuration, exactly. Section VII separately tests weighting itself (equal vs. inverse-volatility) and is the only place inverse-volatility numbers appear in this article.

Textbook diversification: Sharpe rises and CAGR fades as the portfolio dilutes toward the market, from N=10 through N=25. Drawdown improves sharply between N=10 and N=15 (−37% → −26%) and keeps improving more gradually after that. There's no "right" N, just a return/drawdown trade you pick a point on. N=15 was chosen deliberately over N=25 (better Sharpe, ~3 points shallower MaxDD): it keeps more of the concentrated small-cap return, at the cost of a somewhat rougher ride — more return, less smooth.

A regime overlay is doing real work: turning it off costs 23 points of drawdown (−49% vs −26%) for a CAGR gain that doesn't show up in Sharpe (1.240 vs 1.312). MA75 gives the best Sharpe of any window tested here; MA50 gives a similar drawdown but lower Sharpe, and MA100/MA150 converge on almost exactly the same MaxDD as MA75 while losing Sharpe — the filter isn't gaining much by looking further back, just reacting slower. The mcap filter matters less than it first appears under equal weighting: with no filter at all, Sharpe (1.316) is actually in line with the P10–P50 range, not collapsed the way it is under other portfolio constructions we've tested for this universe. What removing the filter does change is CAGR (+37.4%, the highest in this table) and drawdown (−32.9%, meaningfully deeper than any filtered value) — the unfiltered universe is picking up more micro-cap volatility, it just isn't destroying the Sharpe ratio the way it does elsewhere. Inside the P10–P50 range the choice is genuinely low-stakes: Sharpe stays in a tight 1.25–1.33 band throughout. We use a percentile filter rather than a fixed dollar floor specifically because a fixed floor gets stricter every year as the market grows and would need to be manually re-calibrated; a percentile filter doesn't. P30 is kept for the meaningfully shallower drawdown versus no filter, even though the Sharpe case for it alone is weaker than previously stated.

VI. Volatility-Exclusion Filter

Before ranking by momentum each month, we exclude the most volatile slice of the eligible universe (252-day realized volatility, annualized). This is the most granular sweep in this study — ten values from off to 50%:

Sharpe rises from off (0.95) up to roughly 40–50% exclusion (1.55), essentially flat between those two — no sharp isolated spike at one value with performance falling away on both sides, which is the pattern that would be more consistent with curve-fitting. This is a smooth, mostly one-directional risk/return trade instead: excluding more of the most volatile names raises Sharpe and, past 25%, shrinks drawdown too, while CAGR peaks around 10–20% exclusion (34.5–34.7%) and then declines as the filter starts cutting into genuine high-momentum winners, not just noise.

25% was chosen as a mid-point — past the CAGR-maximizing level, short of the Sharpe/MaxDD extreme — explicitly trading some raw return for a shallower drawdown. It is not the single "best" value on any one metric; it's a deliberate point on a well-behaved curve, which is a very different thing from a number that was searched for and happened to fit. The 252-day volatility used here is a trailing window ending at the decision date, applied to the following month's return — same audited timing convention as everywhere else in this study, no lookahead.

This doesn't transfer to our USA/UK momentum strategies as-is. Those already use a high absolute market-cap floor, which strips out most of the small, illiquid, high-volatility names before any volatility filter would get a chance to. Re-running the Canada universe with a comparable absolute floor ($2–5B CAD instead of the P30 percentile) previously made the volatility filter's benefit mostly disappear too — Sharpe uplift from adding it was +0.36 (percentile universe, off→25%, equal-weight) versus roughly +0.03–0.13 in an earlier large-cap-floor test; that large-cap-floor comparison hasn't been independently re-run under equal weighting, so treat it as directional rather than exact. The filter is solving a problem specific to TSX's broader, small-cap-heavy eligible universe, not adding a universal edge — worth remembering before assuming it would help elsewhere.

VII. Weighting Scheme

WeightingSharpeCAGRMaxDD
Equal-weight (final)1.312+33.3%−26.1%
Inverse-volatility1.359+32.4%−24.0%

At N=15, this is a genuine trade-off, not a clean win either way: inverse-vol edges out equal-weight on both risk-adjusted metrics (Sharpe 1.359 vs 1.312, MaxDD −24.0% vs −26.1%), while equal-weight gives up a little of each for almost a full point more CAGR (+33.3% vs +32.4%). Inverse-vol weighting mostly underweights the highest-conviction, highest-momentum names (which tend to be the most volatile), which is exactly what shaves a bit off both the top-line return and the tail risk. The gap between the two is real but small on every metric. Equal weight is the live/final configuration — chosen for simplicity (no second data source, no second thing that can fail) and the extra return, not because it was shown to dominate inverse-vol; a reasonable investor could prefer the other side of this trade.

VIII. Two Filters We Tested and Dropped

Liquidity / ADV floor
A minimum average-daily-dollar-volume filter is not redundant with the market cap filter on the TSX — about half of stocks above a $100M mcap floor fail a $1M/day ADV test in any given year (concentrated, family-owned resource names are common here). But adding it on top of the percentile mcap filter and volatility-exclusion filter changed the backtest by a negligible amount, so it was dropped for simplicity rather than kept as an unnecessary extra knob.
Monthly return cap
An earlier version of the code capped any single month's return at ±50%, on the assumption it was clipping data errors. It wasn't — the largest real held-position return in the backtest was +350% in one month, a genuine small-cap momentum spike, not a bad print. The cap was removed once we confirmed this; a filter shouldn't silently override a real, rare data point.

IX. Data Quality: Two Contamination Checks

CDR contamination
Canadian Depositary Receipts — foreign mega-caps (AAPL, AMZN, GOOGL, MSFT, NVDA, TSLA, META and others) cross-listed on the TSX with huge global market cap but a tiny local float — got selected into the top-15 in 24 of 319 months when left in. Removing them changed CAGR by about 0.1 percentage point. Real, but not distorting; excluded on principle.
Mistagged preferred shares
50 TSX preferred-share series (e.g. bank preferreds like RY-PH) carry EODHD fundamentals data that appears copied from their common-stock parent — RY-PH showed a market cap north of $18B off 1.4 billion "shares," when its real preferred float is a small fraction of that. Zero of these were ever actually selected into the top-15 across the full backtest; excluded by ticker pattern anyway, since there's no argument for letting mistagged instruments into the eligible universe.

XI. Limitations

  1. Skip-month (12-1) vs. no-skip (12-0): at N=15, no-skip wins clearly on Sharpe, CAGR and MaxDD together (see Section IV) — only tested at one portfolio size, and not yet checked out-of-sample or at other N.
  2. Equal weight vs. inverse-volatility (Section VII): a genuine trade-off at N=15 — inverse-vol has a small Sharpe/MaxDD edge, equal-weight has better CAGR. Equal weight is the live config for its simplicity and the extra return, not because inverse-vol was shown to be worse overall — worth re-testing if N ever changes, since an earlier (pre-engine-fix) N=25 test also favored inverse-vol — not independently re-verified under the current engine.
  3. Single-market backtest. Every number here is TSX-only and doesn't necessarily generalize to other markets (see Section VI on the volatility filter: it works in Canada partly because of TSX's small-cap/resource-heavy universe, and doesn't transfer as-is to markets with a higher market-cap floor already).
  4. Sector concentration. The TSX is heavily weighted toward energy and materials; the top-15 momentum book will often be concentrated in commodity-cycle names, visible in the position history on the strategy report.
  5. Drawdown magnitude. −26.1% is a real, live-tradeable number in this backtest, not a tail-risk footnote — this is a high-volatility, high-conviction strategy, not a low-vol product.
