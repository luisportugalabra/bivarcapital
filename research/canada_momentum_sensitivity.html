Canada MomentumParameter sensitivity & how we got to the final config

How each parameter in the Canada TSX momentum strategy was chosen: one-at-a-time sensitivity sweeps across lookback window, market cap filter, portfolio size, regime window, volatility filter and weighting scheme. TSX (.TO), EODHD daily data, 2000–2026.

I. Universe & Method

Universe
Toronto Stock Exchange (.TO), EODHD daily adjusted prices, 1,766 tickers since 2000
Signal
N-month total return (lookback swept below)
Selection
Top K by signal, descending, after all eligibility filters
Rebalance
Monthly, end-of-month signal
Regime filter
TSX Composite (^GSPTSE) close vs its own N-day moving average
Regime rule
Above MA → fully invested / Below → 100% cash
Costs
20bps round-trip on monthly turnover
Period
2000–01 to 2026–07 (319 months)

The engine is audited for lookahead bias: every signal uses only data available as of its decision date, and each month's return is generated by the position decided using the prior month's information, never its own.

One baseline for every table below: N=15, 12-month momentum (no skip), mcap ≥ 30th percentile, excludes top 25% most volatile, MA75 regime, inverse-volatility weighted, CDRs and preferred shares excluded, since 2000 (319 months). Every table changes exactly one of those settings and holds the rest fixed at this same baseline — so the baseline row reads CAGR +31.0%, Sharpe 1.304, MaxDD −39.0% in every single table below. That repeated row is intentional: it's how you can trust that a table's other rows differ only because of the one thing being tested, not because of a silently different setup.

Textbook diversification: Sharpe rises and drawdown shrinks smoothly as N grows, while CAGR fades as the portfolio dilutes toward the market. There's no "right" N, just a return/drawdown trade you pick a point on. N=15 was chosen deliberately over N=25 (materially better Sharpe and MaxDD in this table): it keeps more of the concentrated small-cap return, at the cost of a rougher ride — more return, less smooth.

A regime overlay is doing real work: turning it off costs almost 9 points of drawdown (−48% vs −39%) for a modest CAGR gain that doesn't show up in Sharpe. MA75 gives the best Sharpe of any window tested here; MA50 gives a shallower drawdown but lower Sharpe, and windows above 100 days converge on the same MaxDD as MA75 while losing CAGR and Sharpe — the filter isn't gaining much by looking further back, just reacting slower.

A clean peak at 12 months on both Sharpe and CAGR, degrading on both sides — not a knife-edge value sitting next to a cliff, which is the pattern that would be more consistent with curve-fitting. We also tested 12–1 (skip the most recent month, the standard academic convention to avoid short-term reversal) against pure 12–0: skipping gives a slightly better Sharpe (1.313 vs 1.304) but noticeably lower CAGR (+28.5% vs +31.0%) and a shallower drawdown (−29.5% vs −39.0%). We kept the no-skip version for the extra return, but this is closer than it first looked — the "12-1 is a myth here" claim from earlier drafts of this research was too strong; it's a real trade-off, not a one-sided win, and worth re-checking at other portfolio sizes before treating either choice as settled.

With no market cap filter at all, CAGR looks spectacular (+54.9%) and the Sharpe collapses to 0.23 — the strategy is picking up illiquid micro-caps with huge, erratic swings, not a repeatable edge. Any filter in the P10–P50 range fixes that immediately and the choice inside that range is genuinely low-stakes: Sharpe stays in a tight 1.29–1.37 band throughout. We use a percentile filter rather than a fixed dollar floor specifically because a fixed floor gets stricter every year as the market grows and would need to be manually re-calibrated; a percentile filter doesn't.

VI. Volatility-Exclusion Filter

Before ranking by momentum each month, we exclude the most volatile slice of the eligible universe (252-day realized volatility, annualized). This is the most granular sweep in this study — ten values from off to 50%:

Sharpe rises monotonically from off (0.98) all the way to 50% (1.65) — no peak, no reversal. That's the opposite signature of overfitting: a curve-fit parameter typically shows a sharp, isolated spike at one value with performance falling away on both sides. This is a smooth, one-directional risk/return trade instead: excluding more of the most volatile names monotonically raises Sharpe and shrinks drawdown, while CAGR peaks around 15% exclusion (35.3%) and then declines as the filter starts cutting into genuine high-momentum winners, not just noise.

25% was chosen as a mid-point — past the CAGR-maximizing level, short of the Sharpe/MaxDD extreme — explicitly trading some raw return for a shallower drawdown. It is not the single "best" value on any one metric; it's a deliberate point on a well-behaved curve, which is a very different thing from a number that was searched for and happened to fit. The 252-day volatility used here is a trailing window ending at the decision date, applied to the following month's return — same audited timing convention as everywhere else in this study, no lookahead.

VII. Weighting Scheme

WeightingSharpeCAGRMaxDD
Equal-weight1.309+32.9%−36.4%
Inverse-volatility (final)1.304+31.0%−39.0%

At N=15, equal-weight is marginally better on every metric here, not inverse-vol — the opposite of what an earlier, less concentrated (N=25) test found. This is a real, honest result, not a typo: at N=15 the portfolio is concentrated enough that inverse-vol weighting mostly just underweights the highest-conviction, highest-momentum names (which tend to be the most volatile), giving up some of the return those names are in the portfolio for in the first place. We're keeping inverse-vol in the live config regardless, on the logic that its benefit shows up more reliably at larger N and we'd rather not re-litigate the weighting scheme every time N changes — but this table is reported as-is, including the part that argues against our own choice.

VIII. Two Filters We Tested and Dropped

Liquidity / ADV floor
A minimum average-daily-dollar-volume filter is not redundant with the market cap filter on the TSX — about half of stocks above a $100M mcap floor fail a $1M/day ADV test in any given year (concentrated, family-owned resource names are common here). But adding it on top of the percentile mcap filter and volatility-exclusion filter changed the backtest by a negligible amount, so it was dropped for simplicity rather than kept as an unnecessary extra knob.
Monthly return cap
An earlier version of the code capped any single month's return at ±50%, on the assumption it was clipping data errors. It wasn't — the largest real held-position return in the backtest was +350% in one month, a genuine small-cap momentum spike, not a bad print. The cap was removed once we confirmed this; a filter shouldn't silently override a real, rare data point.

IX. Data Quality: Two Contamination Checks

CDR contamination
Canadian Depositary Receipts — foreign mega-caps (AAPL, AMZN, GOOGL, MSFT, NVDA, TSLA, META and others) cross-listed on the TSX with huge global market cap but a tiny local float — got selected into the top-15 in 24 of 319 months when left in. Removing them changed CAGR by about 0.1 percentage point. Real, but not distorting; excluded on principle.
Mistagged preferred shares
50 TSX preferred-share series (e.g. bank preferreds like RY-PH) carry EODHD fundamentals data that appears copied from their common-stock parent — RY-PH showed a market cap north of $18B off 1.4 billion "shares," when its real preferred float is a small fraction of that. Zero of these were ever actually selected into the top-15 across the full backtest; excluded by ticker pattern anyway, since there's no argument for letting mistagged instruments into the eligible universe.

XI. Limitations

  1. Skip-month (12-1) vs. no-skip (12-0): at N=15, no-skip wins on CAGR and MaxDD, skip wins narrowly on Sharpe (see Section IV) — genuinely close, only tested at one portfolio size, and not yet checked out-of-sample. Treat the no-skip choice as a return-over-smoothness preference, not a settled finding.
  2. Inverse-volatility weighting underperforms equal-weight at N=15 (Section VII) in this specific backtest. It's kept in the live config on the belief that it holds up better at less concentrated N, not because this table supports it directly — worth re-testing if N ever changes.
  3. Single-market backtest. Every number here is TSX-only. The lookahead-bug class this whole study started from was found on Canada specifically; it has not been individually re-checked against every other market in our broader momentum research.
  4. Sector concentration. The TSX is heavily weighted toward energy and materials; the top-15 momentum book will often be concentrated in commodity-cycle names, visible in the position history on the strategy report.
  5. Drawdown magnitude. −39% is a real, live-tradeable number in this backtest, not a tail-risk footnote — this is a high-volatility, high-conviction strategy, not a low-vol product.
