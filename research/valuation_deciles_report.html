Which valuation metrics still predict returns?We tested five. Only two work.

We sorted every US stock into deciles by P/E, P/S, P/B, EV/EBITDA, and Shareholder Yield, then tracked returns for 21 years (2005–2026). To eliminate timing bias, each test was run 12 times — once starting each month — and averaged. The results are definitive.

P/S (+7.4%) and Shareholder Yield (+5.9%) still predict returns.   P/E (+0.0%), P/B (−3.0%), and EV/EBITDA (−0.2%) do not.
D1 minus D10 CAGR spread · 5 metrics · 10 deciles · 12 monthly portfolios averaged · 2005–2026

The Spread

The "value premium" for each metric is measured as the CAGR difference between the cheapest decile (D1) and the most expensive decile (D10). A positive spread means cheap stocks outperform expensive stocks. A negative spread means the metric is inverted — expensive beats cheap.

P/S (Price-to-Sales) has the strongest spread at +7.4% for >$200M stocks. Revenue is the hardest fundamental to manipulate — it doesn't depend on accounting choices, depreciation methods, or one-time charges. Low P/S stocks genuinely generate more revenue per dollar of market cap.

Shareholder Yield (dividends + buybacks) has a +5.4% spread at >$200M and +5.9% at >$5B — the only metric that works better in large caps. Companies returning the most cash to shareholders outperform by nearly 6% annually. This is real capital being returned, not an accounting artifact.

P/B is inverted. At >$5B market cap, the most expensive stocks by P/B outperform the cheapest by 3.0% per year. This makes sense: in a world dominated by intangible assets (software, brands, IP), a high P/B simply means the company has valuable assets that don't appear on the balance sheet. A low P/B means the company's tangible assets are all it has — and those assets are often declining in value.

Implications

For stock pickers

If you're screening for cheap stocks, use P/S and Shareholder Yield. Ignore P/E (zero predictive power), P/B (inverted), and EV/EBITDA (negligible spread). A stock that is cheap on P/S and has high shareholder yield is genuinely undervalued. A stock that is cheap on P/E might just have volatile or cyclical earnings.

For factor investors

The "value factor" as typically constructed (using P/B, as in Fama-French HML) has been negative in this period. If you're building a multi-factor model, replace P/B with P/S or shareholder yield. The academic definition of "value" is outdated.

For quant strategies

Any composite value score (like the VC2) that includes P/B and P/E is diluting the signal from the metrics that actually work (P/S, SY). A two-factor value screen using only P/S and shareholder yield would likely outperform a six-factor composite.

Methodology

Test design
For each metric, sort all eligible stocks into 10 deciles. Equal-weight each decile. Hold for 12 months. Rebalance.
Timing bias elimination
Each test run 12 times (one per calendar month). Results averaged across all 12 start dates. This ensures no single rebalance month drives results.
Total backtests
5 metrics × 2 market caps × 12 months × 21 years = 2,520 portfolio-years
Period
2005–2026 (21 years, out-of-sample for most academic studies)
Universe
US equities, ex-financials, positive metric values only
Market caps
>$200M and >$5B tested
Data
Sharadar / Nasdaq Data Link (17,618 tickers, 12,151 delisted)
Prices
End-of-month adjusted close (dividends reinvested)
Shareholder Yield
Dividend yield + buyback yield (from net cash from common equity)
Outlier treatment
P/E < 200, P/S < 100, P/B < 50, EV/EBITDA < 100
