Which valuation metrics still predict stock returns?We tested seven. Most are broken.

Every US stock sorted into deciles by seven valuation metrics. Tracked for 21 years (2005–2026). Quarterly TTM fundamentals from Sharadar. Monthly and annual rebalance tested independently. The results challenge the foundations of value investing.

The Test

For each metric, we sort all eligible US stocks (ex-financials, positive values) into 10 equal groups (deciles). D1 = cheapest 10%. D10 = most expensive 10%. Each decile is equal-weighted and held for the measurement period. The "value premium" is the return difference between D1 and D10.

If the metric predicts returns, cheap stocks (D1) should outperform expensive stocks (D10) — positive spread. If the spread is zero, the metric is dead. If negative, the relationship has inverted.

We test two rebalance frequencies. Annual rebalance (12 portfolios averaged, one per start month) measures the true factor premium — can you buy cheap, hold for a year, and outperform? Monthly rebalance uses the freshest quarterly data but may capture turnover effects rather than pure factor value.

P/B — The Fama-French Factor Is Broken

Price-to-Book is the foundation of academic value investing — the Fama-French HML factor that launched a thousand papers. In our data, it's inverted: expensive stocks (high P/B) outperform cheap stocks (low P/B) by 2–4% per year. The annual spread is -3.8%. Only 35% of rolling 3-year windows are positive.

D1 (Cheap P/B)
Energy, basic materials, old-economy industrials. Companies whose only assets are factories, inventory, real estate — tangible assets on the balance sheet. These sectors have structurally underperformed.
D10 (Expensive P/B)
Technology, healthcare, communication services. Companies with enormous intangible value — software, IP, brand, network effects — that doesn’t appear on the balance sheet. These have dominated the market since 2010.
In 1990, tangible assets made up 68% of S&P 500 market value. Today less than 10%. A low P/B in 2025 means "this company's only assets are physical" — factories, inventory, real estate in declining industries. A high P/B means enormous intangible value — software, brand, IP, network effects. P/B now separates the old economy from the new one, and the new one wins.

SH Yield — Returning Capital Is a Sell Signal

Shareholder yield (dividends + buybacks) was O'Shaughnessy's improvement to the VC2. In our data, it's the worst-performing factor: -7.4% spread monthly, -5.9% annual. Only 6% of rolling 5-year windows are positive. High SY stocks are mature companies in energy and consumer cyclicals returning capital because they have nothing to invest in. Low SY stocks are tech and healthcare companies reinvesting everything into growth.

Implications

If building a value composite today: use P/S (always), P/FCF (with monthly rebalance), and optionally EV/EBITDA (monthly, large caps). Do not include P/E (dead), P/B (inverted), or SH Yield (strongly inverted). The traditional VC2 includes P/E, P/B, and SH Yield — all three are broken. This is why it underperforms.

Data & Methodology

Fundamentals
Sharadar quarterly (MRQ). Flow items use trailing 12 months (TTM). Balance sheet items use latest quarter. 2-month availability lag.
Decile sort
Equal-weight within each decile. Positive values only per metric. Ex-financials.
Rebalance
Monthly: end of each month. Annual: 12 portfolios (one per start month) averaged.
Period
2005–2026 (21 years, out-of-sample)
Universe
US equities >$200M and >$5B tested
Survivorship
17,618 tickers, 12,151 delisted included
Prices
End-of-month adjusted close (dividends reinvested)
