Buying cheap stocks doesn't work anymore.21 years of evidence.

We sorted every US stock into deciles by four valuation metrics — P/E, P/S, P/B, EV/EBITDA — and tracked returns for 21 years (2005–2026). The cheapest stocks do not meaningfully outperform the most expensive ones. The value premium, as traditionally measured, is dead.

Cheapest decile (D1): +8.9% CAGR  ·  Most expensive (D10): +7.2%  ·  Spread: +1.7%
P/E deciles · >$200M market cap · 2005–2026 · 17,618 stocks · Survivorship-bias free

This contradicts decades of academic research. Fama & French (1992), O'Shaughnessy (1996–2011), and virtually every factor investing textbook claims that cheap stocks (low P/E, low P/B) systematically outperform expensive ones. Our data shows the spread is negligible — roughly 1–2% per year before costs, and in some metrics the relationship inverts entirely.

Why the Value Premium Disappeared

1. Intangible assets changed everything

In 1990, tangible assets (factories, inventory, real estate) made up 68% of S&P 500 market value. Today it's less than 10%. Companies like Apple, Microsoft, and Google have minimal book value relative to their earnings power. Traditional value metrics (P/B, P/S) systematically exclude the best businesses in the modern economy, pushing value portfolios into dying industries.

2. "Cheap" now means "distressed"

In the 1960s–1990s, low P/E stocks were often well-run companies temporarily out of favour. Today, the cheapest decile is dominated by energy companies, brick-and-mortar retail, and legacy industrials facing structural decline. The price is low for a reason.

3. Information is priced faster

When Graham and Dodd wrote Security Analysis in 1934, finding an undervalued stock required reading paper filings. Today, every quantitative fund in the world screens for low P/E stocks simultaneously. The easy alpha has been competed away.

4. Survivorship bias in historical studies

Many studies showing a strong value premium used databases with known survivorship issues. Cheap stocks that went bankrupt were often missing from the data, inflating historical value returns. Our dataset includes 12,151 delisted stocks to avoid this bias.

What This Means for Investors

Buying stocks solely because they're "cheap" on any single valuation metric has not generated meaningful alpha in the past 21 years. This doesn't mean valuation doesn't matter — it means that a low P/E or low P/B alone is not a signal. Value investing requires additional filters (quality, momentum, sector constraints) to separate genuine undervaluation from value traps.
