Negative Momentum + ValueBuying beaten-down cheap stocks. The contrarian trap.

Can you profit from buying the most hated stocks in the market — as long as they're cheap and growing? We tested a contrarian strategy that buys stocks with the worst 12-month momentum, filtered by P/E < 10, positive EPS, and strong earnings growth. 21 years of data. 252 portfolios. The answer is clear.

+5.9% CAGR  vs  S&P 500: +9.4% CAGR
Negative momentum + value · 2005–2025 · 12 monthly portfolios averaged · Survivorship-bias free

This strategy underperforms the S&P 500 by −3.5% per year with nearly double the volatility. Buying the worst-performing cheap stocks is a losing strategy. The stocks are cheap and falling for a reason — and the reasons persist. The strategy only works during post-crash recoveries (2009, 2020) but destroys value the rest of the time. Sharpe ratio: 0.10.

The Thesis

The idea is simple and intuitive: find stocks that are cheap (P/E < 10), profitable (EPS > 0), with growing earnings (EPS growth >10% 1yr, >20% 2yr) — but that the market has punished severely over the past 12 months. The contrarian bet: the market is overreacting, and these stocks will snap back.

This combines two well-documented factors in academic finance: value (cheap stocks outperform) and mean reversion (extreme losers tend to recover). The filters for earnings growth add a quality screen to avoid genuine value traps. In theory, this should work.

In practice, it doesn't.

The Pattern: Only Works After Crashes

The strategy's returns are dominated by two years: 2009 (+79.1%) and 2020 (+74.4%). Both follow massive market crashes where beaten-down stocks snapped back violently. Remove those two years and the strategy's CAGR drops to approximately +1.5% — catastrophic underperformance.

Outside of post-crash recoveries, the strategy averages +0.7% per year vs +8.1% for the S&P 500. This is not a strategy with occasional bad years — it's a strategy that only works in one very specific market regime (post-crash recovery) and fails in all others. You would need to perfectly time when to deploy it, which defeats the purpose of a systematic approach.

Why Negative Momentum Fails

1. Momentum is the strongest factor in finance

Jegadeesh & Titman (1993) demonstrated that stocks with positive momentum continue to outperform, and stocks with negative momentum continue to underperform. This is one of the most replicated findings in financial economics. Betting against momentum is betting against the most persistent market anomaly.

2. Cheap + falling = falling for a reason

A stock with a P/E of 5 and negative momentum isn't a misunderstood bargain — it's usually a business in structural decline. The low P/E reflects the market's expectation that earnings will collapse. The EPS growth filter should help, but trailing EPS growth can be misleading: a company can report great earnings in its most recent year while its forward outlook deteriorates.

3. Value traps are systematic

The entire strategy is a value trap generator by design. It screens for stocks that look cheap on paper but that the market is actively repricing downward. The EPS growth requirement helps marginally, but it cannot overcome the fundamental problem: price momentum contains information about the future that trailing fundamentals do not.

4. Extreme volatility destroys compounding

With 28% annual volatility (vs ~15% for the S&P 500), the strategy suffers from volatility drag. A year of −33.8% requires a +51% gain just to break even. The strategy's best years (+79%, +74%) are impressive in isolation but are consumed by the many years of losses.

Sector Concentration Risk

A critical problem: because the strategy buys the cheapest, most beaten-down stocks, it systematically concentrates in whichever sector is most out of favour. In 2014–2015, that meant energy. In 2018–2019, it was retail and industrials. This creates unintended sector bets that dominate the portfolio's performance.

This is not a diversified strategy. When you buy the 25 cheapest, most hated stocks, you're often buying 15 stocks from the same sector. The portfolio is a concentrated bet on the most unloved corner of the market — which is exactly where the most permanent capital losses occur.

The Verdict

Do not use this strategy. Negative momentum combined with value screens produces inferior risk-adjusted returns to a simple index fund. The strategy has a Sharpe ratio of 0.10 (near zero), almost double the volatility of the benchmark, and underperforms in the majority of years. Its spectacular post-crash recoveries are not repeatable on demand and cannot compensate for the chronic underperformance in normal market conditions.

The academic evidence is clear: momentum works best when you go WITH it, not against it. Jegadeesh & Titman's original finding was about buying winners and selling losers — not the reverse. The contrarian "buy the dip" mentality, while psychologically appealing, is systematically unprofitable when applied to individual stocks.

If you want exposure to cheap, growing stocks, combine value with positive momentum — the trending value approach. Or simply buy an index fund.
