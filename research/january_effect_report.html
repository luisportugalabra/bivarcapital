The January EffectWhen you rebalance matters more than you think.

Across 21 years of data and multiple strategy variants, January rebalancing consistently produces the highest returns. The effect is large, persistent, and has a clear economic explanation.

January rebalance: +12.7% CAGR  ·  July rebalance: +4.9% CAGR
Same VC2 strategy, same stocks, same universe — only the rebalance month differs. Gap: 7.8% per year.

A 7.8% annual difference from timing alone. The VC2 strategy at >$200M market cap returns +12.7% CAGR when rebalanced in January but only +4.9% when rebalanced in July. This is not noise — the pattern persists across multiple strategies, market cap levels, and time periods. The January effect in value stocks is one of the most robust anomalies in finance.

Why January?

1. Tax-loss selling in December

Investors sell losing positions in November–December to realize capital losses for tax purposes. This creates artificial selling pressure on beaten-down value stocks, depressing their prices below fundamental value. When selling pressure ends on January 1, these stocks bounce back sharply.

2. Window dressing by fund managers

Institutional investors "clean up" their portfolios before year-end reporting, selling embarrassing losers and buying recent winners. This pushes value stocks down further in Q4 and creates a reversion opportunity in January.

3. New capital flows

Year-end bonuses, annual IRA/401k contributions, and institutional rebalancing all inject fresh capital into markets in January. Value stocks — already oversold from tax-loss selling — benefit disproportionately from this demand.

4. Structural, not arbitrageable

Unlike most market anomalies, the January effect is driven by tax incentives that can't be arbitraged away. As long as the US tax code allows capital loss harvesting, December selling pressure and January rebounds will persist. The effect has been documented since the 1970s and shows no sign of disappearing.

The January effect is not about "January being a good month for stocks." The S&P 500 has roughly average returns in January. The effect is specific to value stocks — stocks that are cheap and have been selling off. Growth stocks and the broad market don't show the same pattern.

Surprising: Day 15 slightly outperforms Day 1 for the standard VC2 (+13.0% vs +12.6%). The January effect does not concentrate in the first few trading days as commonly assumed. It persists throughout the month, possibly because tax-loss selling recovery is gradual, not instantaneous. However, the best absolute result comes from VC2 without P/B on Day 1 (+13.3%).

Practical Implications

For value investors
• Rebalance in January, not randomly
• The 7.8% gap between Jan and Jul is too large to ignore
• Consider splitting: value rebalance in Jan, momentum in Sep–Oct
• Tax-loss harvest your own portfolio in December to buy cheap stocks in January
Caveats
• Higher turnover from annual rebalance means tax impact
• The effect could weaken if too many investors exploit it
• Some of the January alpha may be compensation for liquidity risk
• Single-month results have wide confidence intervals

Methodology

Test design
12 identical VC2 portfolios, each starting in a different month. Same universe (>$200M US equities, ex-financials, positive earnings), same 25-stock equal-weight portfolio, same annual rebalance period. Only the start month differs.
Period
2005–2026 (21 years)
Seasonality data
Monthly returns from the April-rebalance VC2 portfolio, averaged across 21 years for each calendar month
Data
Sharadar / Nasdaq Data Link, survivorship-bias free (12,151 delisted stocks included)
Day 1 vs Day 15
VC2 >$5B tested with buy/sell on first trading day (Day 1) vs 15th (Day 15) of January
