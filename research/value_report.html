The Value Composite 2does not deliver what the book promised.

O'Shaughnessy's What Works on Wall Street reported 18–21% CAGR for the VC2 strategy over 40+ years of backtested data. We tested it on 21 years of out-of-sample data (2005–2026) with institutional-grade fundamentals from Sharadar. The results are significantly weaker.

Book: 18–21% CAGR  →  Reality: 7.2% CAGR  vs  S&P: 9.0%
VC2 >$200M · April rebalance · 2005–2026 · Survivorship-bias free

The gap between theory and practice is enormous. O'Shaughnessy's backtests covered 1927–2009, a period where value investing consistently outperformed. Our out-of-sample period (2005–2026) includes the post-GFC growth/tech dominance that fundamentally changed which factors generate alpha. The VC2 averaged across all 12 rebalance months delivers just +8.2% CAGR — slightly below the S&P 500's +8.4%.

What the Book Claims

In What Works on Wall Street, O'Shaughnessy backtested the Value Composite 2 on US equities from 1927 to 2009 and reported:

• ~18–21% CAGR for the top decile of the VC2 ranking
• Consistent outperformance over the S&P 500 (~10.9% CAGR) across all decades
• Superior risk-adjusted returns (higher Sharpe ratios) than any single-factor value strategy
• The six-factor composite captures value that individual metrics miss

Why It Underperforms

1. The world changed

The VC2 was optimized on 1927–2009 data. Since 2010, the market has been dominated by capital-light technology companies (Apple, Microsoft, Google, Amazon, Meta) that will never screen as "cheap" on P/B, P/S, or EV/EBITDA. The strategy's universe is structurally shrinking as the economy shifts from tangible to intangible assets.

2. Sector concentration kills

Without sector constraints, the VC2 systematically overweights energy, materials, and cyclicals — they're always the "cheapest." When these sectors crash (2008, 2015, 2019-20), the portfolio has nowhere to hide.

3. Value traps

No quality filter means the portfolio includes genuinely distressed companies heading for bankruptcy. Stocks like Hi-Crush (−93%), Bed Bath & Beyond (−72%), and SunEdison (−97%) were quantitatively "cheap" for structural reasons.

4. Small caps destroy value

At >$200M market cap, value traps dominate. At >$5B, the cheap stocks are more likely to be temporarily mispriced blue chips that mean-revert upward. Market cap is the single most important filter for making VC2 work.
