US + UK + Canada MomentumCorrelation & combined portfolio analysis

We run three live momentum strategies — US Bivar Optimal Momentum, UK Optimal Momentum, and Canada TSX Momentum. This article combines all three 1/3 each and asks the question that actually matters for sizing a multi-strategy book: are they really diversifying, or just three flavours of the same long-momentum bet? III. Combined Portfolio — Dynamic Redistribution

A natural-sounding refinement: instead of letting a leg's capital sit idle in cash/gold when its own regime filter says "exit," redistribute that leg's weight pro-rata to whichever of the other two strategies are currently active. If only one strategy is active in a given month, it gets 100% of capital; if none are active, all three legs simply keep their own defensive return (cash or gold), same as the static version.

Regime overlap across the 302-month window: all three strategies active together in 149 months (49%), two of three active in 64 months (21%), only one active in 51 months (17%), and all three defensive simultaneously in 38 months (13%) — in that last case the dynamic version collapses back to the static one, since there is nowhere to redistribute to.

The dynamic version is a worse portfolio despite the higher CAGR. Sharpe falls (1.76 → 1.53) and MaxDD nearly triples (−15.9% → −42.1%). The mechanism is concentration risk: in the 17% of months where only one strategy is active, that one strategy now carries 100% of the book instead of 33%. And those single-survivor months are not random — they cluster around exactly the periods when the other two strategies' regime filters have already flagged trouble, i.e. elevated-risk periods for momentum generally. Redistributing capital into a concentrated bet during exactly the moments the system is telling you risk is elevated is the opposite of what a regime filter is for. We do not run this version live.

Correlations get more negative, not more positive, in down months. UK–Canada goes from roughly zero (−0.02) in up months to strongly negative (−0.48) in down months; US–UK deepens from −0.27 to −0.42. This is the opposite of the failure mode most multi-strategy books actually have — correlations that look fine in calm markets and then converge toward 1 exactly when a book is losing money. Here, the worse the combined month, the more the three legs are pulling in different directions.

VIII. Stress Test: The Classic Momentum-Crash Window (Apr–Sep 2009)

The GFC row above stops at 2009-03, which understates the real stress test. The best-documented failure mode for cross-sectional momentum isn't the crash itself — it's the V-shaped reversal immediately after, when last year's biggest losers (the short leg of any long-short momentum book) violently outperform. Academic long-short momentum studies show factor losses in the 40–50% range concentrated almost entirely in April–September 2009. If our momentum exposure has a hidden crash risk, this is the specific six months where it would show up.

All three strategies were positive through the window that historically destroys momentum — none of them lost money. Two things are doing the work, and it's worth being precise about which: first, all three books are long-only (buy the top-N winners, no short leg) — the textbook crash mechanism is specifically a short-squeeze on the biggest losers, which cannot hit a portfolio that never holds a short book in the first place. Second, timing: the US (MA250) and UK (MA200) regime filters were still out of the market through April–June, missing the most violent chop of the initial reversal; Canada's much faster MA75 had already re-entered by April and rode the recovery from day one. This isn't the strategies dodging a bullet through skill — it's structural: a long-only book facing a beta-driven junk rally underperforms a long-short book's short-covering losses, it doesn't crash the way the short leg does.

Caveat: this is one historical instance (n=1), not a repeatable stress test. 2009 was an unusually fast, policy-driven V-shaped recovery; there is no second comparable event in the sample to confirm this holds generally. Long-only momentum's immunity to the classic short-squeeze mechanism is a structural argument, not something proven by one data point — treat this section as "no evidence of the specific failure mode," not "proof it cannot happen."

IX. Whipsaw Cost in Choppy, Sideways Markets

Regime filters have a known failure mode in the other direction: markets that chop sideways without a clean trend generate repeated false exit/re-entry signals, each one clipping a bit of return. We checked 2011 and 2015 specifically (flagged as classic whipsaw-prone years), plus 2018, 2019 and 2022 for context, against each strategy's own CAGR — computed over the same 2002–2025 window (full calendar years only) used consistently across this article, not each strategy's own full, differently-dated history.

2011, 2015 and 2018 are real drag years across all three strategies — every single cell in those three rows sits below its strategy's CAGR, several by 27–42 percentage points. This is the whipsaw cost the regime filters genuinely pay in choppy, directionless markets, and it is not a small number in a single-strategy view. 2018 is the worst of the three, not 2011 or 2015 — both US (−16.7%) and UK (−12.4%) posted outright negative years.

We also counted year-by-year regime flips (exit↔re-entry transitions) to check whether flip-frequency itself predicts the damage. It doesn't, cleanly: 2019 has among the highest flip counts for UK (5) and Canada (5) in the entire sample, yet both had strong-to-exceptional years (+24.4% and +48.2%). Meanwhile 2011 (US: 1 flip, UK: 1 flip, Canada: 2 flips) was a below-average year for all three despite low flip counts. What matters isn't how often the filter fires, it's whether each exit caught a real drawdown or clipped a false signal ahead of a continued uptrend — a single badly-timed flip can cost more than five well-timed ones. By flip-count alone, 2018, 2019 and 2022 are actually noisier years than 2011 or 2015 for UK and Canada; it's 2011/2015/2018 together, not those two years specifically, that make up the strategies' weakest historical stretch.

X. Takeaways & Limitations

  1. Equal weight (1/3 each) is the version we'd run. It captures almost all of the diversification benefit (Sharpe 1.76, MaxDD −15.9%) with none of the concentration risk the dynamic-redistribution version introduces.
  2. The negative-skew-in-down-months finding (Section V) is the single most useful number in this article — correlations getting more negative exactly when the combined book is losing money is the property you actually want from a multi-strategy blend, and it isn't guaranteed by low full-period correlation alone.
  3. The strategies show no evidence of the classic long-short momentum crash (Section VIII), for structural reasons (long-only, no short leg) rather than luck — but this rests on one historical window, not a repeated stress test.
  4. Whipsaw cost in sideways markets is real and non-trivial (Section IX) — 2011, 2015 and especially 2018 cost every strategy 25–49pp versus its own long-run average. This is the honest price of the regime filters that produce the crisis protection in Sections VII–VIII; the two are the same mechanism, not separate features.
  5. Crisis-window correlations are individually noisy (as few as 3 months of data in three of the seven windows tested) — treat the direction as informative and the precise coefficient as illustrative, not a statistically robust estimate on its own.
  6. Three markets, three regime filters, no shared design. None of these three strategies were built together or tuned to complement each other — each was optimized independently on its own market (see each strategy's own sensitivity report). The low correlation and negative down-month skew found here are closer to an emergent property of momentum-with-independent-regime-filters across uncorrelated equity markets than a designed feature, which is also why we treat it as a real, if not fully explained, effect rather than something to be suspicious of.
  7. Capital/margin netting not modeled. All figures assume the three books can be run fully independently with no shared capital constraints; a real implementation running all three simultaneously would need to check margin/netting efficiency across brokers and currencies (GBX, CAD, USD).
