US + UK + Canada MomentumCorrelation & combined portfolio analysis

We run three live momentum strategies — US Bivar Optimal Momentum, UK Optimal Momentum, and Canada TSX Momentum. This article combines all three 1/3 each and asks the question that actually matters for sizing a multi-strategy book: are they really diversifying, or just three flavours of the same long-momentum bet? III. Combined Portfolio — Dynamic Redistribution

A natural-sounding refinement: instead of letting a leg's capital sit idle in cash when its own regime filter says "exit," redistribute that leg's weight pro-rata to whichever of the other two strategies are currently active. If only one strategy is active in a given month, it gets 100% of capital; if none are active, all three legs simply stay in cash, same as the static version.

Regime overlap across the 302-month window: all three strategies active together in 158 months (52%), two of three active in 50 months (17%), only one active in 51 months (17%), and all three defensive simultaneously in 43 months (14%) — in that last case the dynamic version collapses back to the static one, since there is nowhere to redistribute to.

The dynamic version is a worse portfolio despite the higher CAGR. Sharpe falls (1.60 → 1.54) and MaxDD grows by more than half (−15.5% → −24.5%). The mechanism is concentration risk: in the 17% of months where only one strategy is active, that one strategy now carries 100% of the book instead of 33%. And those single-survivor months are not random — they cluster around exactly the periods when the other two strategies' regime filters have already flagged trouble, i.e. elevated-risk periods for momentum generally. Redistributing capital into a concentrated bet during exactly the moments the system is telling you risk is elevated is the opposite of what a regime filter is for. We do not run this version live.

VIII. Stress Test: The Classic Momentum-Crash Window (Apr–Sep 2009)

The GFC row above stops at 2009-03, which understates the real stress test. The best-documented failure mode for cross-sectional momentum isn't the crash itself — it's the V-shaped reversal immediately after, when last year's biggest losers (the short leg of any long-short momentum book) violently outperform. Academic long-short momentum studies show factor losses in the 40–50% range concentrated almost entirely in April–September 2009. If our momentum exposure has a hidden crash risk, this is the specific six months where it would show up.

All three strategies were positive through the window that historically destroys momentum — none of them lost money. Two things are doing the work, and it's worth being precise about which: first, all three books are long-only (buy the top-N winners, no short leg) — the textbook crash mechanism is specifically a short-squeeze on the biggest losers, which cannot hit a portfolio that never holds a short book in the first place. Second, timing: the US MA250 regime filter was still in cash through April–June, missing the most violent chop of the initial reversal entirely (flat 0%, not a gain and not a loss); the faster UK (MA200) and Canada (MA75) filters had already re-entered and rode the recovery, contributing the bulk of the combo's +26.4%.

Caveat: this is one historical instance (n=1), not a repeatable stress test. 2009 was an unusually fast, policy-driven V-shaped recovery; there is no second comparable event in the sample to confirm this holds generally. Long-only momentum's immunity to the classic short-squeeze mechanism is a structural argument, not something proven by one data point — treat this section as "no evidence of the specific failure mode," not "proof it cannot happen."

IX. Whipsaw Cost in Choppy, Sideways Markets

Regime filters have a known failure mode in the other direction: markets that chop sideways without a clean trend generate repeated false exit/re-entry signals, each one clipping a bit of return. We checked 2011 and 2015 specifically (flagged as classic whipsaw-prone years), plus 2018, 2019 and 2022 for context, against each strategy's own CAGR — computed over the same 2002–2025 window (full calendar years only) used consistently across this article.

2011, 2015 and 2018 are drag years across all three strategies — every cell in those three rows sits below its strategy's CAGR, several by 20–43 percentage points. 2018 is clearly the worst of the three — US posted its worst year of the whole sample (−19.4%) and UK was also sharply negative (−11.9%). 2019 and 2022, by contrast, were strong years for UK and Canada despite also being sideways/choppy markets by reputation — a reminder that "known whipsaw year" is a much fuzzier label in the data than in the narrative.

We also counted year-by-year regime flips (exit↔re-entry transitions) to check whether flip-frequency itself predicts the damage. It doesn't, cleanly: 2019 has among the highest flip counts for UK and Canada in the entire sample, yet both had strong-to-exceptional years (+28.9% and +48.2%). What matters isn't how often the filter fires, it's whether each exit caught a real drawdown or clipped a false signal ahead of a continued uptrend — a single badly-timed flip can cost more than five well-timed ones.

X. Realistic Max Drawdown — The Fast-Crash Blind Spot

Every regime filter in this article re-evaluates once a month. That's a real blind spot: a crash fast enough to happen inside a month — a 1987-style single-day break, or a weekend gap — is fully invested in all three markets before any filter gets a chance to fire.

If all three posted their own worst-ever active month at once — a reasonable proxy for a fast, gap-driven crash that catches every regime filter still fully invested — the equal-weight combo loses −20.3% in that single month. That is already deeper than the entire backtested multi-month MaxDD of −15.5%. Notably, the worst month actually realized in this sample — −15.5% in February 2026 — already sits close to this hypothetical stress figure, driven by UK and Canada both posting their worst-ever active months simultaneously that same month. All three strategies were simultaneously active, and so simultaneously exposed to this risk, in 158 of 302 months (52%).

For sizing purposes, we'd treat −15.5% to −20% as the realistic range rather than assuming the backtested MaxDD is a hard floor — this sample already includes a month (Feb 2026) that came close to the full simultaneous-worst-case stress estimate, so this isn't a purely theoretical tail risk.

Sharpe ranges 1.42–1.62 across all 27 combinations, and all 27 clear 1.40 — the worst combination in the entire grid still comfortably beats every strategy's own standalone Sharpe (1.01–1.41). The strategies' own windows produce the best result in the grid (1.62), which makes sense — they were each chosen for a reason — but the gap to the worst corner of the grid (1.42) is small, not a cliff. This is a genuine robustness result: the three-way diversification in this article isn't riding on a fine-tuned per-market parameter choice.

Methodology note: this table is built by reconstructing each strategy's pure momentum return (regime removed entirely) and re-gating it post-hoc with each MA window, rather than re-running the full original backtest per combination. The "own windows" row here reads Sharpe 1.62, close to but not identical to the 1.60 reported in Sections II–IX — a small, expected gap from reconstruction-vs-original timing precision, not a discrepancy in the underlying data.

XII. Takeaways & Limitations

  1. Equal weight (1/3 each) is the version we'd run. It captures almost all of the diversification benefit (Sharpe 1.60, MaxDD −15.5% backtested — but see Section X for a more realistic sizing range) with none of the concentration risk the dynamic-redistribution version introduces.
  2. Correlations are moderate and positive, not near-zero (Section IV–V). All three pairs sit around 0.27–0.29 full-period, close to zero specifically in up months, and mixed (not uniformly favourable) in down months. The diversification benefit comes more from the three regime filters rarely all triggering at once (Section VII) than from genuinely unrelated stock-picking.
  3. The strategies show no evidence of the classic long-short momentum crash (Section VIII), for structural reasons (long-only, no short leg) rather than luck — but this rests on one historical window, not a repeated stress test.
  4. Whipsaw cost in sideways markets is real (Section IX) — 2011, 2015 and especially 2018 cost every strategy 20–43pp versus its own CAGR. This is the honest price of the regime filters that produce the crisis protection in Sections VII–VIII; the two are the same mechanism, not separate features.
  5. The backtested MaxDD is close to a realized fast-crash scenario, not a conservative floor (Section X). February 2026 already delivered a −15.5% month with UK and Canada both posting worst-ever active-month losses simultaneously; we'd size to a 15–20% realistic drawdown range rather than treat −15.5% as a ceiling.
  6. The regime-window choice is not fine-tuned overfit (Section XI). All 27 possible MA-window combinations across the three markets keep combined Sharpe above 1.40 — the diversification result doesn't depend on the specific MA250/MA200/MA75 choices, even though those own-market windows do produce the best result in the grid.
  7. Crisis-window correlations are individually noisy (as few as 3 months of data in three of the seven windows tested) — treat the direction as informative and the precise coefficient as illustrative, not a statistically robust estimate on its own.
  8. Three markets, three regime filters, no shared design. None of these three strategies were built together or tuned to complement each other — each was optimized independently on its own market. The moderate, stable correlation found here (Section VI) is closer to an emergent property of momentum-with-independent-regime-filters across related equity markets than a designed feature.
  9. Capital/margin netting not modeled. All figures assume the three books can be run fully independently with no shared capital constraints; a real implementation running all three simultaneously would need to check margin/netting efficiency across brokers and currencies (GBX, CAD, USD).
