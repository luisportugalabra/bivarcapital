Trending Value — P/E SensitivityDoes the P/E threshold matter? We tested five levels.

The trending value strategy buys cheap stocks with positive momentum and strong earnings growth. But how cheap is cheap enough? We swept the P/E threshold from <10 to uncapped across 21 years of data, averaging 12 monthly portfolios per year to eliminate timing bias.

Best: P/E < 25 at +10.4% CAGR  ·  S&P 500: +9.4% CAGR
EPS > 0 · EPS growth 1yr > 10% · 2yr > 20% · Top 25 by positive 12-1mo momentum · 2005–2025

None of the variants produce meaningful alpha after accounting for volatility. The best performer (P/E < 25) returns +10.4% CAGR with +2.6% alpha, but at 23.5% volatility — 50% higher than the S&P. Sharpe ratios range from 0.23 to 0.36. The EPS growth filters help avoid the worst value traps, but don't generate enough edge to justify a standalone strategy.

What We Learned

1. P/E threshold is not the key variable

The difference between P/E < 10 and P/E < 25 is only ~0.2% in CAGR. The spread in Sharpe ratios (0.28–0.36) is narrow. The P/E filter matters far less than you would expect — the EPS growth requirement and momentum ranking do the heavy lifting.

2. Tighter value = lower volatility

P/E < 10 has 20.0% volatility vs 27.7% for no cap. The stricter the value filter, the less volatile the portfolio — because extreme value stocks tend to be more mature, capital-heavy businesses with lower beta. This produces the best Sharpe ratio despite not having the highest raw returns.

3. Removing the P/E cap increases dispersion

Without a P/E cap, the strategy swings wildly: +80.1% in 2020, −32.7% in 2021. This is because high-P/E growth stocks with momentum tend to be the most volatile names in the market. You're buying the fastest-moving stocks regardless of valuation — pure momentum with an EPS growth filter.

4. EPS growth filters are doing the real work

The common element across all five variants is EPS growth > 10% (1yr) and > 20% (2yr). This quality screen ensures you're buying companies with improving fundamentals — not dying businesses that happen to look cheap. The alpha, such as it is, likely comes from this growth filter combined with momentum, not from the P/E threshold.

5. None of these are worth building a product around

A Sharpe of 0.36 at best, with −25% to −33% worst years and only 48–57% win rates against the benchmark. These are not bad strategies per se — they roughly match or slightly beat the S&P 500 — but they do so with significantly higher volatility and drawdowns. An investor needs to ask: is +1–2% alpha worth the stomach-churning ride?
