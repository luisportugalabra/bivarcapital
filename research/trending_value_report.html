Trending ValueValue + momentum. Better, but still not what the book promised.

O'Shaughnessy's "Trending Value" strategy combines the VC2 value composite with 6-month momentum — buying cheap stocks that are also trending upward. In theory, this avoids value traps. Our 21-year out-of-sample test shows some alpha, but far below the book's claims.

Book: ~21% CAGR  →  Reality: +8.0% CAGR  vs  S&P: +8.4%
Trending Value >$200M (original specification) · Avg 12 monthly portfolios · 2005–2026 · Survivorship-bias free

Using the original specification (>$200M market cap, as in the book), the Trending Value strategy does not beat the S&P 500. It returns +8.0% CAGR vs the benchmark's +8.4% — a negative alpha of −0.4%. However, when filtered for large caps (>$5B) and removing P/B from the composite, it improves to +12.2% CAGR with +3.8% alpha. The alpha exists, but only with modifications the book doesn't suggest.

How It Works

Step 1 — Value Screen
Rank all US equities by the VC2 composite (P/E, P/S, P/FCF, EV/EBITDA, Shareholder Yield — without P/B for the best variant). Take the cheapest decile (top 10%).
Step 2 — Momentum Filter
From the value decile, calculate 6-month price momentum for each stock. Select the 25 stocks with the highest momentum.
Step 3 — Hold
Buy equal weight, hold for 12 months, rebalance.

The intuition: pure value buys cheap stocks regardless of whether they're cheap for a reason (falling knife). Adding momentum ensures we only buy cheap stocks that the market has started to re-price upward — confirming that the value thesis is playing out.

The momentum premium depends on the value screen. With the full 6-factor VC2, momentum adds almost nothing at large-cap level. But when P/B is removed (allowing more capital-light growth companies into the value screen), momentum adds +1.7% — a meaningful improvement. This suggests that P/B is the factor most responsible for excluding stocks that momentum would otherwise correctly identify as trending.

Why the Book's Numbers Don't Replicate

1. In-sample vs out-of-sample

O'Shaughnessy's 1927–2009 backtest is in-sample — the strategy was developed using that data. Our 2005–2026 test is partly out-of-sample. Strategies routinely lose 30–50% of their backtested edge when applied to new data.

2. Value premium has declined

The value factor delivered strong returns from the 1930s through the 2000s. Since 2010, growth has dominated. The Fama-French HML (High Minus Low) value factor has been negative or flat in many recent years. This isn't noise — it reflects a structural shift toward intangible-asset businesses where traditional value metrics are less meaningful.

3. Survivorship bias in original data

Older datasets (pre-CRSP, pre-Compustat) have known survivorship issues. Delisted stocks — many of which were "cheap" value stocks that went bankrupt — are often missing from historical databases. Our Sharadar dataset includes 12,151 delisted stocks specifically to avoid this bias.

4. Market cap effect

The book's strongest results come from small/micro-cap stocks that are difficult to trade at scale. When we filter for investable large caps (>$5B), returns drop significantly but remain positive. The "paper" returns of the strategy include stocks with $200M market cap that an institutional investor could never own without moving the price.

The Honest Conclusion

The Trending Value strategy generates real but modest alpha of approximately +3–4% per year over the S&P 500 at large-cap level. In January, the alpha can reach +6%. This is valuable — most active managers fail to beat the benchmark at all.

But it is not the 21% CAGR or the "double your money every 3 years" that What Works on Wall Street suggests. Investors should expect the strategy to:

• Beat the S&P by 3–5% per year on average, before costs
• Underperform in tech-driven bull markets (2017, 2019–2021, 2023)
• Outperform in value rotations (2009, 2013, 2022, 2025)
• Suffer deeper drawdowns than the S&P in crashes (up to −55% vs −37%)
• Require patience through multi-year underperformance periods
