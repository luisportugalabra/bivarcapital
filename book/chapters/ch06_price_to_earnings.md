# Chapter 6 — Price-to-Earnings Ratios

## What the Book Claims

O'Shaughnessy tested P/E from 1964 to 2009 and found:

| Metric | D1 (Low P/E) All Stocks | All Stocks Universe |
|---|---|---|
| **CAGR** | **16.25%** | 11.22% |
| **Sharpe** | **0.61** | 0.33 |
| **$10,000 →** | $10,202,345 | $1,329,513 |

The lowest P/E decile earned nearly $9 million more than the universe over 46 years. Base rates were extraordinary: D1 beat the universe 92% of rolling 5-year periods and 99% of rolling 10-year periods.

For Large Stocks, D1 delivered 13.56% CAGR vs 10.20% for the universe. Sharpe of 0.50 vs 0.32.

The highest P/E stocks (D10) returned just 5.53% — worse than T-bills. O'Shaughnessy concluded that "buying high PE stocks, regardless of their market capitalization, is a dangerous endeavor."

## The Verdict

**P/E is dead as a stock selection factor.**

The book reported a +5% annual spread between cheap and expensive P/E stocks. Our data shows a spread of +0.5% at >$200M and **negative** at >$5B. The D1 barely beats D10 in a coin flip — 52% of years. Rolling 5-year windows are positive only 47% of the time at >$200M and 35% at >$5B. This is indistinguishable from random noise.

At >$5B, cheap P/E stocks actually **underperform** expensive ones by 0.3% per year. The most widely used valuation metric in the world has zero predictive power in the last 21 years.

## Why It Broke

1. **Earnings are the most manipulated number on the income statement.** One-time charges, restructuring costs, stock-based compensation, tax benefits — all distort EPS. A low P/E can mean genuinely cheap, or it can mean temporarily inflated earnings about to revert.

2. **The relationship between P/E and subsequent returns has become non-monotonic.** In the book's data, returns declined smoothly from D1 to D10. In our data, the middle deciles often outperform both extremes.

3. **Period matters.** The book's sample (1964–2009) included long periods where value investing dominated (1970s, early 2000s). Our sample (2005–2026) is dominated by growth/tech. P/E may be cyclical, not permanently broken — but 21 years is a long drought.

4. **The signal-to-noise ratio of P/E is too low.** Other metrics (P/S, P/FCF) capture cheapness more cleanly because they're based on less manipulable fundamentals.

## O'Shaughnessy's Conclusion vs Ours

| | O'Shaughnessy (1964–2009) | Our Results (2005–2026) |
|---|---|---|
| D1 CAGR | 16.25% | 9.0% (>$200M) |
| D1 vs Universe | +5.03% alpha | -1.0% alpha |
| Spread D1-D10 | ~10.7% | +0.5% |
| Base rate (5yr) | 92% | 47% |
| Verdict | "One true faith" | Dead |

The factor that O'Shaughnessy called the foundation of value investing has produced zero alpha in the last 21 years. This doesn't mean P/E is permanently useless — it may recover if the market rotates back to fundamentals-driven pricing. But for the period 2005–2026, buying low P/E stocks was a coin flip.
