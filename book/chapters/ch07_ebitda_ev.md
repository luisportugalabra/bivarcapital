## The Verdict

**The "best single value factor" is now indistinguishable from random.**

At >$200M, the spread is literally 0.0%. Cheap EBITDA/EV stocks and expensive ones returned the same thing over 21 years. At >$5B there's a marginal +0.6% spread — far from the +5.4% the book reported.

The factor that beat the universe 100% of rolling 10-year periods in the book now wins only 47% of rolling 5-year windows — worse than a coin flip.

## Why It Broke

1. **EBITDA ignores stock-based compensation.** In 1964, SBC was negligible. In 2025, tech companies pay billions in SBC that doesn't show up in EBITDA. This makes tech stocks look cheaper than they are on EV/EBITDA, distorting the ranking.

2. **Enterprise value calculation has edge cases.** Companies with large cash positions (tech) have artificially low EV, making their EBITDA/EV look high. Companies with debt (industrials, energy) have higher EV, making them look expensive even if earnings are strong.

3. **Capital structure neutrality is now a bug, not a feature.** O'Shaughnessy praised EBITDA/EV for being "neutral to capital structure." But in a zero-interest-rate era (2009–2022), capital structure stopped mattering. Heavily leveraged companies performed fine because debt was cheap. The metric's advantage disappeared with its raison d'être.
